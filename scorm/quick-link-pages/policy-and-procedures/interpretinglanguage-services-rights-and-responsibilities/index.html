Interpreting, language services rights and responsibilities

Policy Name – Language Services
Policy Number
Version Number
 
 
Scope

All staff and contractors employed by Bagwan Health, Allied Health professionals, Credentialed Specialists and students should be aware of these policies and procedures and be trained in how to access and effectively use language services.

 
Policy 

Bagwan Health requires all interpreters or translators, whether they are Australian Institute of Interpreters and Translators (AUSIT) members or not, to adhere to the AUSIT Code of Ethics and Code of Conduct (November 2012).

 

In summary, the code obliges members to:

·         Maintain professional detachment, impartiality, objectivity and confidentiality

·         Decline work beyond their competence

·         Strive for excellence through continuous regular professional development

·         Promote working conditions, relationships and an understanding of roles that facilitate collaboration and quality service delivery

 

·         Adhere to dispute resolution procedures.

This policy defines the standards and processes associated with working with interpreting and translating services. It is intended to support staff at all levels to provide quality language services to patients and thereby respond to the needs of Victoria’s diverse community, including refugees and asylum-seekers, migrants and people using sign language.

Procedure

Interpreting Services

Interpreting is a highly specialised skill involving the precise, effective and timely translation of information from one language to another. Including an interpreter in a consultation can be beneficial where the patient prefers to speak, or is more fluent, in a language other than English, or the patient’s English skills are inadequate for the consultation.

Why Engage an Interpreter?

Clear and effective communication underpins every aspect of good practice. It is linked to the patient’s satisfaction, adherence to instructions, and health outcomes. Ineffective communication can result in limited, delayed, inefficient care, leading to more costly treatment and intervention, as well as negatively impacting the patient’s understanding of, and trust in, the healthcare system at large.

Engaging interpreters has been found to:

·         Decrease communication errors

·         Increase the patient’s comprehension

·         Reduce unnecessary tests and treatments

·         Improve clinical outcomes

·         Raise the quality of care to the same level as that for people without language barriers

·         Improve the patient’s satisfaction with understanding self-care and follow-up plans, reducing errors and better adherence to treatment

·         Improve the healthcare provider’s satisfaction

Failure to determine the need for, and engage, an interpreter may be considered to be a breach in duty of care.

Informed Consent

Engaging an interpreter is essential for obtaining informed consent from patients with limited English proficiency or Deaf patients from migrant and refugee backgrounds.

Consent for any procedure requires the patient to be:

·         Fully informed about the underlying illness or disease, the nature of the procedure, the consequences of not having the procedure, the degree of certainty about the outcome, the time for recovery and the cost

·         Able to raise any concerns about the risks of particular relevance to them.

At a minimum, you should:

·         Assess the patient’s understanding of the information provided to consent to treatment, taking into consideration both the complexity of the issues and the person’s English language proficiency

·         Engage an interpreter for people with limited English language proficiency.

Consent may not be valid if it is obtained through the person’s family members, partners or friends.

Accessible Interpreter Services

Patients must be informed of the interpreting services that are available to them when accessing health care, as well as collecting medications from a pharmacist and that services are:

·         Provided by certified interpreters

·         Free for the patient

·         Confidential.

Assessing the Need for an Interpreter

Whenever possible, the need for an interpreter should be determined prior to an appointment. Assessing how well a patient understands English is the first step in identifying the need for an interpreter.

Even when a patient appears to have adequate English proficiency in an everyday conversation, stressful or unfamiliar situations may affect their ability to communicate effectively. In addition, the patient may have gaps in confidence and competence between understanding what is being said and being able to ask questions in English.

In addition, Deaf people from migrant and refugee backgrounds will require an Auslan/English interpreter (if they use Auslan) or a Deaf and Auslan/English interpreter (if they use another sign language).

You should engage an interpreter if:

·         Requested by the patient

·         The patient cannot comprehend or respond to basic questions in English

·         You are experiencing difficulty understanding your patient or your patient can only respond in a limited way

·         The patient relies on family or friends to translate

·         The patient prefers to speak in their own language

·         The patient speaks English as a second language and is in a stressful, complex or unfamiliar situation.

Always err on the side of caution when assessing whether an interpreter is needed.

Determining the Level of English Proficiency

To determine a patient’s level of English language proficiency, you may ask the following:

·         Why are you here today?

·         Is English your first language?

·         In which country were you born?

·         How long have you lived in Australia?

·         How long have you been learning or speaking English?

If the patient’s responses are difficult to understand or they cannot respond fluently, an interpreter should be booked.

Determining the Patient’s Preferred Language

A patient’s country of birth cannot be reliably used to determine their preferred language and dialect. To determine a patient’s preferred language, you may:

1.    Ask for their preferred language and dialect, if the patient speaks sufficient English.

2.    Use visual aids that list languages and dialects so that the patient can point to the language they speak.

3.    Contact a language service provider to assist you in identifying the language through a telephone interpreter.

What if an Interpreter is Refused? 

If a patient refuses the offer of an interpreter, you should:

·         Address the patient’s concerns (e.g. they may think there will be a charge)

·         Discuss the risks associated with misunderstandings or miscommunication

·         Inform them that engaging an interpreter is of benefit to Bagwan Health as much as it is to them, and that family members can still be involved as support persons.

If the patient still refuses an interpreter, then you must document the discussions and the language support that will be provided to the patient in the patient’s medical record.

Mode of Interpreting

Interpreting services are provided in three ways:

·         Onsite (face-to-face)

·         Telephone

·         Video remote.

Where possible, the patient’s preference for an onsite, telephone or video remote interpreter should be respected.

Onsite Interpreting

Onsite interpreters attend in person and are useful for:

·         Lengthy, complex situations

·         Legally binding matters

·         Facilitating a better understanding of information by taking into account non-verbal communication such as body language.

Onsite interpreting must usually be booked in advance. Most language service providers have a minimum fee for onsite interpreting, based on a 90-minute session. In rural and regional areas the availability of onsite interpreters in some languages may be limited.

Telephone Interpreting

Telephone interpreters attend via telephone and are useful for:

·         Short, non-complex matters

·         Determining the patient’s preferred language and the nature of an enquiry before an onsite interpreter is booked

·         Accessing an interpreter in rural and regional areas, when video conferencing is not available, and in less common languages in which there are a limited number of accredited interpreters

·         When immediate assistance is required and before an onsite interpreter is booked.

As telephone interpreters cannot take into account non-verbal communication, they are not used for Auslan patients. However, calls to someone who is Deaf can be made using an ordinary handset through the National Relay Service on 133 677.

Pre-booking a telephone interpreter is not required, although this should be confirmed with the language service provider.

Video Remote Interpreting

Video remote interpreting uses video conferencing systems, laptop and/or tablets to provide face-to-face communication between a patient and an off-site interpreter.

Video remote interpreters are useful for:

·         Patient’s in rural or regional areas

·         When a local Auslan interpreter is not available

·         Where an interpreter is required at short notice.

Video remote interpreting is a potentially cost-effective alternative to onsite interpreting but requires a good internet connection and suitable equipment to provide the speed and data capacity required.

Understanding the Role of Family and Friends

There are potential ethical, professional and legal consequences and significant adverse outcomes to allowing family, friends or intimate partners to facilitate interpretation. Personally involved individuals should never be used to act in the role of an accredited interpreter as they may:

·         Provide inaccurate or inadequate interpretation as they do not have the required language competence

·         Withhold or distort information due to a lack of impartiality

·         Undermine your confidence that the necessary information is being communicated appropriately

·         Not adhere to the same standards of conduct as accredited interpreters

·         Be placed in compromising or confidential situations

·         Be upset by the experience.

Family and friends can have an important and helpful role supporting and advocating for the patient, which should be acknowledged, valued and understood as completely separate from and complementary to the role of an interpreter. Engaging a qualified interpreter enables family and friends to focus on their advocacy and support role.

Children under 18 years of age should never be asked to act in the place of an accredited interpreter or to relay information between you and a patient. It poses a number of ethical dilemmas and it is also not uncommon for minors from migrant and refugee backgrounds to be fluent in English but not necessarily in their parents’ first language or languages.

Lastly, in some instances using a family member to act in the role of an interpreter may jeopardise the safety of the patient (e.g. in the case of family violence).

Documenting the Need for an Interpreter

The need for an interpreter should be clearly and visibly documented in the patient management system. Information should include:

·         Whether an interpreter was provided when requested or identified as needed

·         The patient’s preferred language

·         The patient’s preferred gender and/or religion of the interpreter, if applicable.

This information should be used to alert colleagues of the need to request an interpreter for all future consultations.

Booking an Interpreter

The following information will be required when booking an interpreter:

·         Patient’s name

·         Language/dialect required

·         Preferred gender and/or religion of the interpreter (if relevant)

·         Date and time the interpreter is required – allow time to brief the interpreter beforehand

·         Type of appointment

·         Address

·         Name and telephone contact details of the staff member who will be conducting the session

·         Nature of the matter to be discussed

·         Anticipated length of the session

·         The interpreter’s name, if a specific interpreter is required for continuity of care reasons.

For some patients, the gender and religion of the interpreter will be important. Prior to booking an interpreter, you should ask the patient if he or she has a preference.

Knowing the name of the interpreter prior to the session is also useful in identifying any concerns the patient or interpreter may have.

If booking a telephone interpreter, inform the language service provider about the telephone system that will be used e.g. is it a speakerphone, or will you and patient be using a dual handset telephone?

Requests for a Preferred Interpreter

The use of a patient’s preferred interpreter ensures that the patient is comfortable with and trusts the interpreter, and helps the communication process. This is particularly important for ongoing or sensitive sessions. Requests for a preferred interpreter can be made at the time of booking. Preferences will be observed as long as the interpreter is available and has the required professional level qualification for the situation.

If an Interpreter is not Available

In the event that an interpreter is not available, you are responsible for assessing the risks of proceeding with the session without an interpreter as opposed to the risk of rescheduling the appointment to allow time to engage an interpreter.

In case of an emergency and when an interpreter is not available, this must be noted in the patient’s record and an interpreter should be engaged as soon as possible to ensure accurate information is communicated.

Preparing for the Session

Prior to the commencing the session, brief the interpreter on general background information (e.g. the nature of the consultation), specific terms to be used (e.g. medical words) and what needs to be achieved. Depending on the duration of the session, you may also consider setting breaks for long sessions.

If the session is being conducted onsite, you must ensure the room is set up appropriately.

Consideration should be given to appropriate lighting, glare from windows and seating arrangements. Ideally, seats will be arranged in a triangle with the patient facing you and the interpreter being next to the patient.

Avoid leaving the interpreter alone with the patient, either in the room where the session will take place or in a waiting room.

If the session is being conducted via telephone or video remote, you must ensure you have access to the appropriate technology and understand how to work the telephone/IT system. Telephone interpreting is more effective when a landline is used rather than a mobile phone. It is also recommended that a telephone with a speakerphone (or two handsets) be used where possible.

Working with Interpreters

Interpreters relay the messages uttered by all parties as accurately as possible, without adding or omitting content or attempting to correct the person’s style or grammar. This includes maintaining the person’s tone of voice, style and manner of speech when interpreting into English, as well as maintaining the register of all parties’ utterances.

Do not request or expect interpreters to:

·         Assess any of the patient’s functional abilities, except for their linguistic abilities

·         Make a judgment as to the competence of the patient or establish the patient’s understanding

·         Act as a chaperone for clinical examinations

·         Act as a professional with bicultural or bilingual skills, a carer or a health advocate for the patient.

 

If the interpreter recognises cross-cultural misunderstanding, or comprehension or cognitive difficulties on the part of the patient, the interpreter may raise it with you to allow you to take appropriate steps, such as rephrasing, clarifying or asking further questions.

 

 

Working with Auslan Interpreters

 

As with all interpreting, you should speak directly to the patient and not the interpreter.

 

Auslan interpreters work in a similar way to other language interpreters. The interpreter will typically sit or stand next to the service provider and opposite the patient. This allows the Deaf person to maintain eye contact with the speaker, read body language and engage with the person speaking whilst receiving the message from the interpreter.

 

Two interpreters may take turns in signing, depending on the length of the session.

 

It is common for Deaf people to request a specific interpreter or agency, as generally Deaf people are familiar and work regularly with Auslan interpreters. This request should be accommodated where possible.

 

 

 

During the Session

At the start of the session:

·         Introduce yourself and the interpreter to the patient

·         Explain the role of the interpreter will interpret what is being said and that they are bound by confidentiality and must maintain impartiality

·         Explain the purpose of the session and what you hope to achieve

·         Do not assume that the patient knows what the session is about

·         Explain to the patient that questions or concerns can be raised at any time during the interview.

During the session:

·         Talk directly to the patient, not the interpreter, and maintain eye contact, and appropriate facial expressions and body language.

·         Use the first person when speaking to your patient. For example, say “When did you eat last?” instead of “When did she eat last?”

·         Speak clearly and use plain English. Explain complex concepts or terminology and avoid using slang, jargon, acronyms, colloquialisms and metaphors.

·         Speak at a reasonable speed and make one point at a time. Pause at the end of a sentence and avoid overlapping speech, so as to provide time for the interpreter to understand and remember what is being said and to interpret in stages.

·         Allow the interpreter to clarify information with you – if there is a need to clarify, ask the interpreter to explain this to the patient first.

·         Allow the patient to ask questions or raise issues at any time.

·         if you have any questions about the patient’s cultural background, ask the patient directly and not the interpreter.

·         In a multidisciplinary team consultation, take turns and allow adequate pauses to facilitate good quality and accurate interpretation.

·         Summarise the discussion occasionally to ensure understanding.

·         Do not ask the interpreter to edit your information to suit the patient’s background.

·         When working with a telephone interpreter, use a speakerphone or a hands-free telephone.

·         When working with a telephone or video interpreter, interact directly with the patient and ensure they manage turn-taking, and use adequate descriptive language.

At the end of the session:

·         Summarise key points for the patient

·         Check that the patient understands any information you have conveyed and if there are any final questions

·         Allow the interpreter to leave separately from the patient.

After the session:

·         Debrief the interpreter and discuss any issues experienced during the session that relate to the role of the interpreter

·         Raise any unprofessional or unsatisfactory practices directly with the interpreter

·         Do not ask for or allow the interpreter to express an opinion about the patient or what they have said.

Complaints and Feedback

If there are issues with the performance of an interpreter, discuss with the interpreter in the first instance. If not resolved, contact the relevant language service provider to make a formal complaint.

Other issues such as the interpreter not attending, charging incorrect fees or interpreters with inappropriate levels of accreditation, can also be discussed with the language service provider.

Translation Services

Translating is a specialised skill that involves the written communication of the meaning of a source-language text to an equivalent target-language text. Translated information is a great way to communicate with patients, their families and carers, and provide them with information that they can refer to later. Translated materials do not replace the need for interpreting services.

Translation services are used to translate patient-specific information, such as medical documents, and also to distribute health and service information to a broader community audience for information and educational purposes.

Prior to Translation

When the intent is to distribute health and service information to a broader community, it is integral to ensure the resource meets the needs of the service area, meets timelines, remains within budget, avoids duplication and is delivered to a high standard. This can be done by identifying planning and consultation.

Use multiple data sources such as the Australian Bureau of Statistics, Census data, recent settlement data, and service usage data to identify your target audience and gain an accurate picture of the diversity in the community.

Preferred Language

Consult with your target audience to determine their preferred language and level of literacy. In some countries, several languages may be spoken and it is important to determine which one is preferred by the local migrant community. Dialect and regional variations should also be considered when selecting language/s for translation.

In addition, consult with your target audience regarding the appropriate writing system for the target language/s. Differing writing systems may be used within particular languages e.g. some Chinese languages use traditional characters whilst others use simplified character sets.

Other Communication Options

Written translation should be avoided for communities where the majority of members have low literacy levels, e.g. refugees and asylum seekers may not be able to read or write as a result of limited or disrupted education opportunities.

Alternative ways of providing multilingual information can include:

·         Information sessions, seminars or presentations using accredited interpreters

·         Audio information, such as podcasts, CDs or DVDs, or recorded messages

·         Storyboards, including images, photos, diagrams or charts

·         Easy English, which uses clear, simple language and possibly diagrams to make information as simple as possible without changing the meaning.

Use consultation methods to discover the communication preferences of your target audience and the best way for them to disseminate your information. This can include asking focus groups, other organisations who work with the community, local committees and community groups.

Avoiding Duplication

As a guiding principle and to avoid duplication, translated resources should be shared across organisations and services. Before making a decision to translate, check available translation directories or other organisations’ websites to ensure a similar resource does not already exist.

If it does not, contact other organisations and explore developing the resource together. By sharing the cost, organisations can often develop a more comprehensive resource.

Writing for Translation

Once you have identified the message to be conveyed and confirmed that translated material is the best way to communicate that message, it’s important to prepare the text as the source content will rarely be suitable for direct translation.

The following tips will assist in preparing a text for translation: 

·         Clarity

o   Use plain English

o   Write in the active voice

o   Explain unfamiliar concepts and complex terms

o   Do not use idioms, metaphors, acronyms, professional jargon, slang, colloquialisms, euphemisms and figures of speech.

·         Brevity

o   Keep it short

o   Consider a summary for translation rather than the full publication.

·         Appropriateness

o   Adapt the source text to each community group

o   Consider the level of pre-existing knowledge in the target group

o   Be alert to cultural sensitivities about particular topics, words, images or graphics

o   Consult the community about the most appropriate way to deal with the topic.

·         Accuracy

o   Proofread the text prior to submitting it for translation and ensure it is final

o   Assume the translator will translate everything as provided – they will not be responsible for correcting errors

o   Use an editor to ensure the clarity and accuracy of the source text.

Arranging a Translation

Before the language service provider can provide a quote for the translation, they will need to know:

·         Name of the organisation

·         Purpose of the translation

·         The audience including ethnic community, age and gender

·         How the translated material will be published or broadcast

·         Any technical requirements such as the computer operating system and software.

·         Additional information including deadlines and copyright arrangements, if applicable.

Once you have engaged a translating service, the translator will require:

·         The text that will be translated, including a glossary of key terms

·         The desired languages (including dialects) for translation

·         The required accreditation level specialisation of the translator

·         The need for the translation to be typeset

·         Translation of the glossary of key terms for future reference

·         Certification of the translation if required

·         Confidentiality requirements

·         Any elements of the text that do not need to be translated (such as logo, names).

Finalising the Translation

All translations must be independently checked by a different translator with at least the same level of accreditation as the original translator. The language service provider can arrange this for an additional fee. Independent checking ensures quality and accuracy and involves comparing the translation with the original text.

Depending on the document, the translated resource should also be reviewed by the target community. This will help identify any particular words or expressions in the translated resource that are unclear or culturally inappropriate. Consider paying communities for their time.

Always publish online the translated version alongside the English version or print both together. This allows an English-speaking user to refer to the translated version with a non-English speaking patient.

Conduct periodic maintenance on translated materials to ensure the materials remain relevant and current. Make sure that the translated information is updated if the original English version is changed.

Lastly, assess the effectiveness of the translated publication by consulting with target communities, specifically requesting feedback on the publication, and/or conduct surveys of the target audience and relevant service providers.

Written Translations by Translators vs. Sight Translations by Interpreters

Some patients from migrant and refugee backgrounds may be unable to read or write in English but some may also be illiterate in their own language. This can make filling out forms or questionnaires very challenging.

 

On-site interpreters should not be asked to provide written translations of material or to fill out forms or questionnaires on behalf of a patient. Interpreters are not always certified as translators and thus, cannot provide official written translations of documentation. If possible, such materials should be translated in advance by a certified translator.

 

If it is necessary for a patient to fill out a written document, or for you to inform a patient of the contents of a document, you should read it to the patient through the interpreter and then complete the form according to the patient’s answers as conveyed by the interpreter.

 

When absolutely necessary, an on-site interpreter may provide sight translation of written information, thereby transferring a message written in English into a message delivered orally in another language. Sight translations must take place in your presence.

 

Sight translation should be used sparingly and only for brief documents (200 – 300 words). Interpreters should not be required to sight translate consent forms, complex medical reports or documents with extensive information on conditions, procedures, options of treatment and risks. Long, complex or technical documents are not suitable for sight translation and will need written translation.
